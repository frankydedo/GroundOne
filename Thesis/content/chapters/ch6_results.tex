This chapter reports what GroundOne achieves on the three frozen test sets, how it compares with the baselines, how
well its probabilities are calibrated and how its latency grows with the number of decisions. It ends with an analysis
of the errors that remain.

\section{Training the 0.5B model}
\label{sec:results-training}

Training behaved as intended. Before training, the model in the marker format answers ``contradicted'' for 88\,\% of
the development sentences and reaches a three-way balanced accuracy of 0.32, which is chance. The training loss falls
below the value of a model that learned only the class frequencies ($\ln 3 \approx 1.10$) within the first 75 steps,
and the development score then rises steadily before levelling off (Figure~\ref{fig:results-dev}). The balanced
accuracy, averaged over the three tasks, reaches 0.55 after 150 steps, 0.63 after 450 and 0.633 at step 600, the best
checkpoint, after which it stays flat. All three classes are predicted throughout, so the model never collapses onto a
single answer. Data-to-text improves the most, while question answering and summarisation settle around 0.57 and 0.51.

\section{Accuracy on the RAG test set (T1)}
\label{sec:results-t1}

On the 17{,}284 sentences of T1 the 0.5B model reaches a three-way balanced accuracy of 0.769. The recall is 0.825 for
supported, 0.675 for contradicted and 0.806 for not-mentioned sentences. On the binary decision it reaches a balanced
accuracy of 0.815 and an AUROC of 0.905; with the lenient policy, which counts only contradictions as errors, the
balanced accuracy is 0.805. These numbers are well above the two cues that never verify a sentence against its
document: word overlap reaches an AUROC of 0.737, and the sentence-only classifier a three-way balanced accuracy of
0.570.

The results differ by task (Table~\ref{tab:results-t1}). Data-to-text is the easiest, with 0.812, and this is also
where contradictions are frequent and well recognised (recall 0.84). In question answering and summarisation the model
separates supported from not-mentioned sentences well, but it recognises few contradictions: 0.03 of the 31
contradicted sentences in question answering and 0.13 in summarisation. These two tasks are nonetheless the clearest
evidence that the model reads the document, because there the sentence-only classifier stays at chance (0.36 and 0.35)
while GroundOne reaches 0.589 and 0.544.

Accuracy does not degrade with the length of the answer. The balanced accuracy is 0.723 for answers with fourteen or
more decisions and the AUROC 0.908, close to the overall values; the hardest band is the shortest one, which mostly
contains question-answering and summarisation answers. This matters for the thesis, because long answers are exactly
where a System One saves the most time compared with a generative judge.

\section{The external benchmark (T2)}
\label{sec:results-t2}

On LLM-AggreFact the 0.5B model reaches a macro-averaged balanced accuracy of 70.1 over the eleven datasets, against
64.2 for the same model used zero-shot and 72.8 for the zero-shot 7B model on the public leaderboard. On the four
datasets closest to retrieval-augmented generation the average is 74.4, the same as MiniCheck-RoBERTa evaluated in the
same way (74.6), and on the RAGTruth column it is 82.1 against 76.5. This last comparison favours GroundOne, which was
trained on RAGTruth training answers while MiniCheck was not, so the fair comparison is the RAG average, where the two
are equal. Outside retrieval-augmented generation GroundOne is weaker than the encoder, by eight to nine points on the
news-summary datasets AggreFact-CNN and XSum, on FactCheck-GPT and on REVEAL. This is the expected cost of training
only on RAG data.

\section{Transfer to a different domain (T3)}
\label{sec:results-t3}

On VitaminC the three-way balanced accuracy is 0.442, above chance (0.33) but far from the result on T1. The model
predicts ``not mentioned'' for 82\,\% of the pairs, recognises only 27\,\% of the supported claims and 11\,\% of the
refuted ones, and its AUROC of 0.722 barely exceeds the 0.698 of word overlap. The gap is not explained by the
different share of classes: the class-weighted training removes the preference for ``supported'', and the AUROC, which
does not depend on base rates or thresholds, is itself weak. Section~\ref{sec:results-errors} shows what the model is
missing.

\section{Calibration and selective prediction}
\label{sec:results-calibration}

The raw probabilities underestimate support. In the reliability diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:results-reliability}) the raw
curve lies well above the diagonal, because the class weights train the model as if the classes were balanced, and the
binary ECE on T1 is 0.30. The temperature and bias fitted on the development set ($T = 0.92$, biases $-2.76$ for
contradicted and $-2.35$ for not mentioned) bring it to 0.019, reduce the negative log-likelihood from 0.55 to 0.21 and
the Brier score from 0.30 to 0.10, while the AUROC stays at 0.905. The map fitted on development data therefore
transfers to the test set of the same domain.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/results/g1-05b-reliability.png}
\caption{Reliability of $P(\text{supported})$ before (raw) and after calibration on the development set, for the
development set, T1 and T3. Points are bins with at least 20 sentences.}
\label{fig:results-reliability}
\end{figure}

Calibration has a consequence that must be kept in mind. The bias restores the true frequency of each class, so the most
probable class becomes ``supported'' more often: accuracy rises from 0.82 to 0.93, but the three-way balanced accuracy
falls from 0.77 to 0.63. Decisions and probabilities therefore serve different purposes. When the three kinds of error
matter equally, decisions are taken on the raw output or with a threshold chosen on development data. When a probability
is used as a confidence, for a policy threshold or to decide which sentences to defer, the calibrated value is the right
one. Used in this way, the calibrated probabilities make selective prediction effective: deferring the least confident
sentences lowers the error rate of the binary decision quickly, with an area under the risk--coverage curve of 0.015 on
T1 (Figure~\ref{fig:results-risk}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{images/results/g1-05b-risk_coverage.png}
\caption{Risk--coverage curves of the binary decision with calibrated probabilities: error rate as the least confident
sentences are deferred.}
\label{fig:results-risk}
\end{figure}

Calibration does not transfer to VitaminC, where the binary ECE rises from 0.17 to 0.21. The bias encodes the base rate
of RAGTruth, where about 88\,\% of sentences are supported, while VitaminC is balanced by construction. A calibration is
valid for the distribution it was fitted on, so a deployment needs a small labelled sample of its own data.

\section{Error analysis}
\label{sec:results-errors}

The weak result on VitaminC and the missed contradictions on T1 have a common cause, which word overlap reveals. On
VitaminC, when a supported claim shares few words with its evidence (overlap below 0.4) the model recognises it in only
2\,\% of cases, against 64\,\% when the overlap exceeds 0.7, and it never recognises a contradiction in a claim without
digits. The model relies partly on lexical overlap: a paraphrased claim looks ``not mentioned'', and a small change to
a copied sentence, often a number, goes unnoticed.

The same weakness exists on T1, with less weight. Where a sentence shares few or some words with the document, the
three-way balanced accuracy is 0.77, but for sentences that copy more than 70\,\% of their words from the document it
falls to 0.54, with a recall of 0.24 for contradicted and 0.43 for not-mentioned sentences. Hallucinations hidden in
sentences that otherwise copy the source are the main blind spot of the model. VitaminC is not harder in absolute
terms: it consists mostly of such cases, paraphrased support and minimal edits, which RAGTruth contains in smaller
numbers.
