This chapter reports how GroundOne learns, what it achieves on the three frozen test sets, how it compares with the
baselines, how well its probabilities are calibrated and how its latency grows with the number of decisions. It ends
with an analysis of the errors that remain.

\section{Training}
\label{sec:results-training}

Both models learned the task. Before training, a model reading the marker format has no notion of what the marker asks
and its verdicts are at chance: the 0.5B answers ``contradicted'' for 88\,\% of the development sentences and reaches a
three-way balanced accuracy of 0.32. In both runs the training loss fell below the value of a model that learned only
the class frequencies ($\ln 3 \approx 1.10$) within the first few dozen steps, and the development score then rose
steadily (Figure~\ref{fig:results-dev}). The 0.5B, trained on all 13{,}203 answers, reached its best score at step 600
of 802, with a balanced accuracy of 0.633 averaged over the three tasks, after which it stayed flat. The 3B, trained for
12.25 hours on 6{,}500 answers, reached 0.705 at step 320 of 403 on its development subset. Measured on the full
development set, the three-way balanced accuracy is 0.731 for the 0.5B and 0.784 for the 3B. All three classes were
predicted throughout both runs, so neither model collapsed onto a single answer.

\section{Accuracy on the RAG test set (T1)}
\label{sec:results-t1}

On the 17{,}284 sentences of T1 the 0.5B model reaches a three-way balanced accuracy of 0.769 and the 3B model 0.832
(Table~\ref{tab:results-t1}). On the binary decision the 0.5B reaches a balanced accuracy of 0.815 and an AUROC of
0.905, and the 3B 0.874 and 0.940. Both are far above the cues that never verify a sentence against its document: word
overlap reaches an AUROC of 0.737, and the sentence-only classifier a three-way balanced accuracy of 0.570.

Three observations stand out. First, accuracy does not degrade with the length of the answer: answers with fourteen or
more decisions are among the best handled, with 0.723 for the 0.5B and 0.839 for the 3B, and the hardest band is the
shortest, which mostly contains question-answering and summarisation answers. This matters because long answers are
where a System One saves the most time. Second, contradictions are recognised mainly in data-to-text, where RAGTruth
concentrates them. In question answering and summarisation the 0.5B separates supported from not-mentioned sentences
well but finds almost no contradictions, with a recall of 0.03 and 0.13. The 3B raises both to 0.30, so model size
compensates in part for the scarcity of such examples in the training data. Third, question answering and
summarisation are the clearest evidence that the models read the document: there the sentence-only classifier stays at
chance (0.36 and 0.35), while the two models reach 0.59--0.70 and 0.54--0.63.

\section{Comparison with the baselines}
\label{sec:results-baselines}

Figure~\ref{fig:results-comparison} compares all systems on T1. The specialised encoders reach an AUROC of 0.814
(MiniCheck-RoBERTa) and 0.821 (HHEM), above word overlap but well below both GroundOne models. The comparison by task
qualifies this result: GroundOne's advantage comes mostly from data-to-text, where it is trained in domain and where
the encoders are weakest (AUROC 0.71--0.75), while on summarisation MiniCheck-RoBERTa is better than the 0.5B model
(0.872 against 0.775) and level with the 3B (0.873). The untrained models confirm that specialisation, not the readout mechanism, produces the
accuracy: the 0.5B is at chance because it never answers ``supported'', and the 3B, asked the same question, reaches
0.437 against 0.818 after training on the same answers.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/results/comparison_t1.png}
\caption{T1: AUROC of $P(\text{supported})$ for every system (left), and three-way balanced accuracy for the systems
with a three-way output (right). Rizzo-Flow is evaluated on 300 answers and the untrained 3B on 600.}
\label{fig:results-comparison}
\end{figure}

The general-purpose System One is the most instructive comparison. On the same 300 answers of T1, Rizzo-Flow, a 4B
model fine-tuned for generic decisions, reaches a three-way balanced accuracy of 0.603, against 0.798 for the 0.5B
GroundOne and 0.840 for the 3B; its AUROC (0.894) is close to that of the 0.5B (0.916) because it ranks sentences well
but predicts ``supported'' for 90\,\% of them. A specialist eight times smaller therefore outperforms the generalist on
the task it was built for.

On LLM-AggreFact (T2) the 0.5B model reaches a macro-averaged balanced accuracy of 70.1 over the eleven datasets,
against 64.2 for the same model used zero-shot and 72.8 for the zero-shot 7B model on the public leaderboard. On the four
datasets closest to retrieval-augmented generation the average is 74.4, equal to MiniCheck-RoBERTa (74.6); on the
RAGTruth column it is 82.1 against 76.5, a comparison that favours GroundOne because it was trained on RAGTruth training
answers. Outside retrieval-augmented generation GroundOne is weaker than the encoder by eight to nine points on
AggreFact-CNN, AggreFact-XSum, FactCheck-GPT and REVEAL, the expected cost of training on RAG data only.

\section{Transfer to a different domain (T3)}
\label{sec:results-t3}

On VitaminC the picture reverses. The 0.5B reaches a three-way balanced accuracy of 0.442 and the 3B 0.527, with
AUROCs of 0.722 and 0.817, while Rizzo-Flow reaches 0.712 and 0.909 on the same 600 pairs and MiniCheck-RoBERTa an AUROC
of 0.968. Two facts put these numbers in context. MiniCheck-RoBERTa starts from the AlignScore model, whose training
data include VitaminC~\cite{zha2023alignscore}, so T3 belongs to its training distribution, whereas for GroundOne it
measures transfer from RAGTruth. Moreover, the gain of the 3B over the 0.5B on T3 comes from model size rather than
from the RAG training: the untrained 3B already reaches 0.531 and an AUROC of 0.821. Specialisation on RAG answers
therefore improves the RAG task substantially but does not, by itself, produce a general fact checker.
Section~\ref{sec:results-errors} shows which cases are missed.

\section{Calibration and selective prediction}
\label{sec:results-calibration}

The raw probabilities underestimate support. In the reliability diagram (Figure~\ref{fig:results-reliability}) the raw
curve lies above the diagonal, because the class weights train the model as if the classes were balanced; the binary
ECE on T1 is 0.30 for the 0.5B and 0.18 for the 3B, whose raw probabilities are already better calibrated. The
temperature and bias fitted on the development set bring the ECE on T1 to 0.019 for both models and reduce the negative
log-likelihood from 0.55 to 0.21 (0.5B) and from 0.36 to 0.17 (3B), while the AUROC is unchanged. The map fitted on
development data therefore transfers to the test set of the same domain.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/results/g1-05b-reliability.png}
\caption{Reliability of $P(\text{supported})$ of the 0.5B before (raw) and after calibration on the development set,
for the development set, T1 and T3. Points are bins with at least 20 sentences.}
\label{fig:results-reliability}
\end{figure}

Calibration has a consequence that must be kept in mind. The bias restores the true frequency of each class, so the most
probable class becomes ``supported'' more often: for the 0.5B on T1 the accuracy rises from 0.82 to 0.93, but the
three-way balanced accuracy falls from 0.77 to 0.63. Decisions and probabilities therefore serve different purposes. When
the three kinds of error matter equally, decisions are taken on the raw output or with a threshold chosen on development
data; when a probability is used as a confidence, for a policy threshold or to decide which sentences to defer, the
calibrated value is the right one. Used in this way, calibrated probabilities make selective prediction effective:
deferring the least confident sentences lowers the error rate of the binary decision quickly, with an area under the
risk--coverage curve of 0.015 (0.5B) and 0.010 (3B) on T1 (Figure~\ref{fig:results-risk}).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{images/results/g1-05b-risk_coverage.png}
\caption{Risk--coverage curves of the binary decision of the 0.5B with calibrated probabilities: error rate as the least
confident sentences are deferred.}
\label{fig:results-risk}
\end{figure}

Calibration does not transfer to VitaminC, where the binary ECE rises from 0.17 to 0.21 for the 0.5B and from 0.14 to
0.19 for the 3B. The bias encodes the base rate of RAGTruth, where about 88\,\% of sentences are supported, while
VitaminC is balanced by construction. A calibration is valid for the distribution it was fitted on, so a deployment needs
a small labelled sample of its own data.

\section{Latency as a function of K}
\label{sec:results-latency}

Figure~\ref{fig:results-latency} shows the central measurement of the thesis: the time needed to verify one answer, on
the same 200 answers of T1, as the number of decisions grows. GroundOne is the fastest system at every size and in every
band of $K$, and its advantage grows with $K$. For answers with fourteen or more decisions, the 0.5B GroundOne needs
0.41 seconds, against 0.80 for the terse judge and 2.51 for the verbose judge of the same size, which is 2.0 and 6.1
times faster; the 3B GroundOne needs 2.57 seconds, against 4.44 and 15.86, which is 1.7 and 6.2 times faster. Reading
all verdicts in one pass is also faster than asking one question per sentence in a batch, by a factor of 1.4--1.5 at
the highest $K$. Model size is the other lever: the 0.5B GroundOne verifies an answer about six times faster than the
3B (0.33 against 1.99 seconds at the median).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{images/results/latency_vs_K.png}
\caption{Median time per answer as a function of $K$ on the same 200 T1 answers (in-process MLX, one answer at a time,
M4 with 16\,GB), for GroundOne, the System One with one question per sentence, and the terse and verbose generative
judges. Logarithmic scale.}
\label{fig:results-latency}
\end{figure}

Speed is not the only difference (Figure~\ref{fig:results-judges}). The untrained generative judges are also much less
accurate and do not always respect the output format. The 0.5B verbose judge produces a parsable verdict for only 3\,\%
of the sentences, because it mostly copies the answer back, and the 0.5B terse judge fails for 23\,\%. The 3B judges
respect the format far better (0.1\,\% and 7.6\,\% failures) but reach a three-way balanced accuracy of only about 0.40,
against 0.90 for the 3B GroundOne on the same answers. The verbose 3B judge writes on average 248 tokens per answer,
all of which must be generated before the verdict can be used, while GroundOne generates none.

\section{Error analysis}
\label{sec:results-errors}

The weak result on VitaminC and the missed contradictions on T1 have a common cause, which word overlap reveals. On
VitaminC, when a supported claim shares few words with its evidence (overlap below 0.4) the 0.5B recognises it in only
2\,\% of cases, against 64\,\% when the overlap exceeds 0.7, and it never recognises a contradiction in a claim without
digits. The models rely partly on lexical overlap: a paraphrased claim looks ``not mentioned'', and a small change to a
copied sentence, often a number, goes unnoticed.

The same weakness exists on T1, with less weight. Where a sentence shares few or some words with the document, the
three-way balanced accuracy of the 0.5B is 0.77, but for sentences that copy more than 70\,\% of their words from the
document it falls to 0.54, with a recall of 0.24 for contradicted and 0.43 for not-mentioned sentences. The 3B reduces
this blind spot without removing it: on the same sentences its recall is 0.36 and 0.56, and its balanced accuracy 0.63.
Hallucinations hidden in sentences that otherwise copy the source remain the main limitation of both models. VitaminC is
not harder in absolute terms: it consists mostly of such cases, paraphrased support and minimal edits, which RAGTruth
contains in smaller numbers.
