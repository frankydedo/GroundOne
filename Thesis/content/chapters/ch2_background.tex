This chapter reviews the work on which GroundOne builds and with which it is compared. The cost of inference on a local
device, which motivates the design, was treated in Chapter~\ref{sec:local}. The chapter starts from System One models, the
idea of reading decisions from a language model instead of generating them, and the rapidly growing ecosystem of decision
models. It then reviews the problem of hallucination in retrieval-augmented generation and the methods that verify
answers against their sources, and closes with calibration, parameter-efficient fine-tuning and the gap that this thesis
addresses.

\section{System One models}
\label{sec:bg-systemone}

The term \emph{System One model} was introduced by TypeSafe with Jev, a hosted model that answers closed questions about
a given state with typed outputs, such as a boolean, a choice among options or a score, without generating
text~\cite{typesafe2026jev}. The name evokes the fast, intuitive mode of thinking described by Kahneman, as opposed to
deliberate reasoning. The vendor reports latencies of tens to hundreds of milliseconds and large cost savings with
respect to generative judges, but these figures are claims, and the architecture is not disclosed.

Open implementations followed, and they differ in how the probabilities are produced. The most common family keeps the
language-model head and reads the logits of the option letters at the answer position, after a prompt that presents the
question as a multiple choice. SemIf applies this readout to a frozen 4B model and reports that 21 criteria about one
document are answered in about one second against more than five seconds for the best generative baseline that produced
valid JSON~\cite{semif}. Rizzo-Flow fine-tunes a 4B model with LoRA on typed decisions, serves the same interface as Jev
and reports gains in accuracy and calibration after fine-tuning~\cite{rizzoflow}; it is the general-purpose System One
used as a baseline in this thesis. A second family encodes the state and each candidate answer separately and compares
their embeddings: Contrastive Language Models are very fast when candidates are many and reusable, but in their own
comparison they agree with Jev's decisions much less than a model that lets state and candidate interact~\cite{kwok2026clm}.
A third family uses small encoders with a dedicated decision head. Laya, for example, scores each option at its own mask
token of a ModernBERT encoder of about 400M parameters; the questions of a request are scored in one batched call, but
each question is encoded with its own copy of the context~\cite{convai2026laya,azizi2026labels}.

The evidence for these systems is uneven. Most of it comes from their authors, often on a single machine and a single
benchmark. Independent evaluations are recent and less favourable to the small open models: an evaluation of Jev on 37
datasets finds its choice probabilities well calibrated, but its binary probabilities, while ranking well, poorly placed
with respect to a fixed threshold of 0.5, and reports a mean balanced accuracy of 78.6 on LLM-AggreFact~\cite{deusser2026jev}; an independent reproduction of Laya finds a different calibration from the one claimed
and a strong dependence on a temperature fitted to the target data~\cite{nandakishore2026laya}; contrastive benchmarks
with clear protocols place small open models far behind Jev~\cite{yong2026decidebench}; and typed decision models have
been shown to follow the label of an option rather than its definition when the two conflict~\cite{azizi2026labels}. In
2026 the Hugging Face hub introduced a tag for these \emph{decision models}~\cite{hf2026decisionmodels}, which in
October 2026 already grouped about 1{,}500 models from some 600 authors, of very different quality.

Within weeks of Jev's release, established organisations published open decision models of their own. Cloudflare's Clef
adds a dedicated decision head to a decoder and answers all questions in a single pass over the state, the design that had
only been inferred for Jev~\cite{cloudflare2026clef}; Liquid AI's d1-3B reads restricted answer letters from a 3B model
whose state is shared by all questions~\cite{liquid2026d1}; Perplexity fine-tunes a 27B model with a letter
head~\cite{perplexity2026decider}. A community leaderboard, the Decision Index, scores these models on a common suite of
public benchmarks that includes RAGTruth at the level of whole answers~\cite{decisionindex2026}. Its results draw a clear
line: general-purpose decision models with fewer than one billion parameters barely exceed the trivial baseline on
RAGTruth, and only models of tens of billions of parameters detect hallucinated answers reliably. Studies of Jev itself
add that its probabilities are calibrated when pooled over many tasks but not on each task separately, that thresholds
and temperatures must be fitted on the target workload~\cite{guo2026justaskjev,li2026jevjudge}, and that choosing a
threshold through a lower confidence bound makes a decision-only judge safer to deploy~\cite{li2026jevjudge}. Runtimes
followed: llama.cpp now serves the System One interface natively~\cite{llamacpp}. None of these models, however, is
specialised in groundedness, and none reports sentence-level, three-way verification on the benchmarks used in this
thesis.

The letter readout itself has been studied outside the System One literature. In multiple-choice evaluation, the
probability of the first answer token and the answer that an instruction-tuned model actually writes often
disagree~\cite{wang2024myanswerc}, and the readout is biased towards particular option identifiers, a bias that can be
estimated and removed~\cite{zheng2024mcq}. Both findings concern zero-shot models and suggest that a letter readout used
as a decision needs to be trained, calibrated, or both. No open System One has been specialised in groundedness
verification and compared, under controlled conditions and on the same hardware, with generative judges, specialised
verifiers and a general-purpose decision model of the same size.

\section{Retrieval-augmented generation and hallucination}
\label{sec:bg-rag}

Retrieval-augmented generation gives a language model access to documents it was not trained on: a retriever selects
passages relevant to the question, and the model writes its answer with these passages in its
context~\cite{lewis2020rag}. The approach is the standard way to build assistants over private documents, and it can
run entirely on a local machine. It does not, however, remove hallucination. Surveys distinguish errors of
\emph{factuality}, with respect to world knowledge, from errors of \emph{faithfulness}, with respect to the given input or
context~\cite{huang2025hallucination}; a RAG answer can be unfaithful to the very passages it was given, by contradicting
them or by adding information they do not contain. RAGTruth documents how frequent this is: it collects about 18{,}000
answers written by six language models in three retrieval-augmented tasks and annotates, at the level of character
spans, the parts that conflict with the context or are not supported by it~\cite{niu2024ragtruth}. Its distinction
between conflicting and baseless content corresponds to the classes \emph{contradicted} and \emph{not mentioned} used
in this thesis.

\section{Groundedness verification}
\label{sec:bg-verification}

Verifying an answer against its source is closely related to natural language inference, the task of deciding whether a
premise entails, contradicts or is neutral towards a hypothesis. Large inference datasets such as
MultiNLI~\cite{williams2018mnli} and the adversarial ANLI~\cite{nie2020anli} provide training data, and
VitaminC~\cite{schuster2021vitaminc} adds contrastive pairs in which a minimal revision of a Wikipedia sentence flips the
verdict, forcing a model to look at the exact fact rather than at word overlap. Applying these models to long generated
texts requires decomposition: SummaC splits both document and summary into sentences and aggregates sentence-level
inference scores~\cite{laban2022summac}, and FActScore decomposes long generations into atomic facts and verifies each of
them~\cite{min2023factscore}. Sentence-level verification, as in GroundOne, is a middle ground: sentences are natural
units of an answer, a user can act on a flagged sentence, and the number of decisions grows with the length of the
answer.

Two families of verifiers dominate practice. Specialised encoders are small and fast. AlignScore trains a single
alignment function on a large mixture of tasks~\cite{zha2023alignscore}; MiniCheck trains small checkers on synthetic data
designed to require reasoning over several sentences and matches much larger models on grounding
benchmarks~\cite{tang2024minicheck}; HHEM is an openly released hallucination evaluation model of the same
kind~\cite{vectara2024hhem}. These models score each pair of document and sentence independently and return a single
probability of support. The second family uses a language model as a judge~\cite{zheng2023judging}. RAG evaluation
frameworks such as RAGAS~\cite{es2024ragas} and ARES~\cite{saadfalcon2024ares} rely on judges, Lynx fine-tunes a judge
specifically for hallucination detection~\cite{ravi2024lynx}, and Self-RAG trains the generator itself to emit critique
tokens~\cite{asai2024selfrag}. Judges are flexible, but their cost grows with what they write, small models often break
the required output format, and grammar-constrained decoding is needed to guarantee a parsable
answer~\cite{willard2023outlines}. LLM-AggreFact collects eleven human-labelled grounding datasets under a common binary
protocol and is the reference benchmark for both families~\cite{tang2024minicheck,llmaggrefact}.

\section{Calibration and selective prediction}
\label{sec:bg-calibration}

A probability is calibrated when, among the predictions made with a given confidence, the share of correct ones matches
that confidence. Modern neural networks are often miscalibrated, and a single temperature applied to the logits, fitted on
held-out data, is a simple and effective correction~\cite{guo2017calibration}; the expected calibration error, computed
over bins of confidence, is the usual measure~\cite{naeini2015ece}. For prompted language models, a per-class bias can
also be needed to correct a systematic preference for some answers~\cite{zhao2021calibrate}. Language models know to some
extent what they know: their token probabilities on closed questions are often informative about
correctness~\cite{kadavath2022know}. When the probabilities are not accessible, the confidence can be asked for in words;
for large models fine-tuned with human feedback this verbalised confidence can be better calibrated than the
probabilities estimated from samples~\cite{tian2023justask}, but systematic evaluations find verbalised confidence
coarse and overconfident, with values concentrated on a few round numbers~\cite{xiong2024uncertainty}. A calibrated
probability becomes useful through selective prediction: a system acts on its confident decisions and defers the
others, and the risk--coverage curve shows how the error rate falls as more decisions are deferred~\cite{geifman2017selective}.

\section{Parameter-efficient fine-tuning}
\label{sec:bg-peft}

Specialising a pretrained model does not require updating all its weights. Low-rank adaptation freezes the model and
trains, for selected weight matrices, a low-rank update that can be merged into the weights or kept as a small separate
adapter~\cite{hu2022lora}; applying it to all linear layers matters more than a high rank, and it combines with quantised
base weights to fit large models in little memory~\cite{dettmers2023qlora}. The Qwen2.5 family provides open models from
0.5B parameters upwards with a shared tokenizer, which makes it possible to compare model sizes with the same
recipe~\cite{qwen2024qwen25}. Fine-tuning small datasets is, however, known to be unstable: results vary with the random
seed through both the initialisation of new parameters and the order of the training data~\cite{dodge2020finetuning};
some runs fail entirely, a behaviour linked to optimisation details such as the absence of bias correction in
Adam~\cite{mosbach2021stability}; and clipping the norm of the gradient is the standard defence against occasional
exploding updates~\cite{pascanu2013difficulty}.

\section{Research gap}
\label{sec:bg-gap}

The pieces needed for a local, fast and calibrated verifier exist separately: compact open models and runtimes that run
them on a laptop, the System One idea of reading decisions instead of generating them, specialised verifiers, and
post-hoc calibration. What is missing is an independent and controlled study that puts them together for one task. In
particular, no work has measured whether a compact System One specialised in groundedness verification can match
generative judges and dedicated encoders on that task, where its advantage comes from, among specialisation, the readout
and the model size, whether its probabilities are good enough to set thresholds and defer decisions, and how its time
and energy compare with those of a judge on the same consumer hardware. The rest of this thesis addresses these
questions.
