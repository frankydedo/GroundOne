# Frantar 2023 – GPTQ: Accurate Post-Training Quantization for Generative Pre-trained Transformers

**Raw file**: `raw/local_inference/Frantar 2023 - GPTQ, Accurate Post-Training Quantization for Generative Pre-trained Transformers (arXiv 2210.17323)_ingested.pdf`

## Overview
A one-shot, **post-training, weight-only** quantisation method based on approximate second-order information. GPTQ compresses GPT-style models to **3–4 bits per weight** with negligible accuracy loss and quantises OPT-175B or BLOOM-176B in about **four GPU hours** on one A100 (abstract, §1). Prior post-training methods at this scale (round-to-nearest, as in LLM.int8() and ZeroQuant) stayed accurate only at 8 bits. With a custom quantised-matrix × fp16-vector kernel, the 3-bit OPT-175B runs on a single 80 GB A100 and generates tokens **3.25× faster** than fp16 on A100s and **4.5×** faster on A6000s (abstract, Table 6). The paper states plainly that the speedup comes from **less memory movement**, not from fewer computations (§6).

## Key Insights
1. **Method in plain words (§3–4, Alg. 1).** Each linear layer is quantised to minimise the squared error of its output on calibration inputs, ‖WX − ŴX‖² (Eq. 1). It builds on Optimal Brain Quantization (OBQ): quantise one weight, then adjust the not-yet-quantised weights to compensate, using the inverse Hessian H = 2XXᵀ. OBQ costs O(d_row·d_col³). GPTQ makes it scale with three changes: (a) quantise all rows in the **same column order**, so the inverse Hessian is updated once per column, cutting cost by a factor min(d_row, d_col); (b) **lazy batch updates** over blocks of B = 128 columns, because the naive update is memory-bandwidth-bound on GPUs (about an order of magnitude faster in practice); (c) a **Cholesky** reformulation plus 1 % dampening for numerical stability on large models.
2. **Setup (§5).** Calibration: 128 random 2,048-token segments of C4 (generic text, no task data). Grid: per-row asymmetric min–max. Baseline RTN uses exactly the same grid, i.e. the weight quantisation of LLM.int8(). Quantisation is done block by block, feeding inputs through the already-quantised blocks.
3. **Quantisation time (Table 2, §5).** OPT-13B 20.9 min, OPT-175B 4.2 h; BLOOM-1.7B 2.9 min, BLOOM-3B 5.2 min, BLOOM-176B 3.8 h (one A100). Small models take under a minute vs about an hour for OBQ.
4. **Accuracy (Tables 3–5, WikiText2 perplexity).** OPT-175B: fp16 8.34; 4-bit RTN 10.54 vs **GPTQ 8.37**; 3-bit RTN 7.3e3 (collapse) vs **GPTQ 8.68**. Small models degrade much more: OPT-125M fp16 27.65 → 4-bit GPTQ 31.12 (RTN 37.28); OPT-1.3B 14.63 → 15.47 (RTN 48.17), 3-bit GPTQ 20.97. BLOOM-3B: fp16 13.48 → 4-bit GPTQ 14.20 (RTN 14.76), 3-bit 17.40 (RTN 39.36). "Larger models generally appear easier to quantize" (§5, Fig. 1), with OPT-66B as an exception.
5. **Grouping and extreme bits (§5, Table 5, 7).** Group size 1024 (≈0.02 extra bits) improves perplexity by about 0.2 and g128 (≈0.15 extra bits) by another 0.1 at 3 bits on the 175B models. At ≈2.2 bits (g128) the loss on OPT-175B is under 1.5 points; ternary weights with g8 reach 9.20.
6. **Speed and memory (§5 "Practical Speedups", Table 6).** 3-bit OPT-175B ≈ 63 GB including fp16 embeddings and output layer, plus ≈ 9 GB of KV cache at 2,048 tokens: fits one 80 GB A100 (fp16 needs 5, LLM.int8() needs 3). Per-token latency at batch 1, 128 generated tokens: A100 230 ms → **71 ms** (3.24×, 5 → 1 GPU); A6000 589 ms → **130 ms** (4.53×, 8 → 2 GPUs). Rationale given by the authors: generation is dominated by **matrix–vector products, which are limited by memory bandwidth**; the kernel dequantises weights on the fly and needs no activation quantisation. The cheaper, lower-bandwidth A6000 gains more.
7. **Limitations (§1, §6).** No speedup for the multiplications themselves (no fp16 × int4 hardware support); no activation quantisation; evaluation centred on perplexity and a few zero-shot tasks; bias effects of compression not studied (Ethics statement).

## Relevance to GroundOne
- **Weight-only quantisation speeds up decode, not prefill.** GPTQ's own explanation of its speedup is the [[Prefill and Decode]] cost model: decode is matrix–vector and bandwidth-bound, so fewer bytes per weight means faster tokens. Prefill is matrix–matrix and compute-bound; a weight-only kernel still multiplies in fp16 after dequantising, so it cannot remove FLOPs (§6: "does not lead to computational reductions"). GroundOne's design (b) costs one prefill and zero decode steps, so moving it from 8 to 3–4 bits would mainly save **memory**, not latency. This is an inference from the paper's mechanism, not a GroundOne measurement.
- **The generative judges are the ones that would profit.** GroundOne's baselines that write verdicts or rationales spend most of their time in decode ([[GroundOne Results]]); on the M4's 120 GB/s, decode is bounded below by weight bytes / bandwidth ([[Apple Silicon and MLX]]). Lower-bit weights would shrink that floor for the judges and therefore **narrow** GroundOne's speed advantage over them. An honest comparison holds the bit width fixed across systems (all GroundOne systems use the same 8-bit bases).
- **Small models are the hard regime.** Tables 3–4 show the relative damage of 3–4-bit quantisation grows as models shrink (OPT-1.3B 14.63 → 15.47 at 4 bits with GPTQ, 48.17 with RTN). Qwen2.5-0.5B is below every OPT/BLOOM size tested except OPT-125M/350M and BLOOM-560M. This supports keeping GroundOne at **8 bits** rather than chasing 4-bit savings, especially because the output is a calibrated probability, not free text ([[Calibration]], [[Letter-Token Readout]]).
- **Post-training vs trained-on-quantised.** GPTQ quantises a frozen model after training and calibrates on generic C4. GroundOne trains its LoRA adapter **on top of the already-quantised 8-bit base** and serves the same base ([[LoRA]], [[Dettmers 2023 - QLoRA]]), so there is no train/serve quantisation mismatch for the adapter; temperature, bias and threshold are fitted on the served model. Switching to a GPTQ/AWQ base would require retraining or at least recalibrating, since the calibration fingerprint includes the base repository.
- **Not directly usable on the M4.** GPTQ's kernels are CUDA (A100/A6000). On Apple silicon, the relevant formats are MLX affine quantisation and GGUF ([[Quantization]]). The paper is cited for the mechanism, not as a runtime option.
