# Fortune 2026 – OpenAI Says It Cracked Navier–Stokes; Buckmaster Accusation

**Raw file**: `raw/local_inference/Fortune 2026 - OpenAI says it cracked Navier-Stokes, Buckmaster accusation_ingested.md`

## Overview
The lead essay of Fortune's *Eye on AI* newsletter of **Tuesday 8 September 2026**, written on the day of OpenAI's Navier–Stokes announcement. It covers the rumours, OpenAI's claim, Tristan Buckmaster's public statement (including his question about his **Codex** sessions and his allegation of intimidation), the replies of Sébastien Bubeck, and Terence Tao's critique of AI labs "strip-mining" open problems. It mixes reporting with the author's **opinion** ("it sounds plausible", "frankly, ridiculous"). The rest of the newsletter (DeepMind genome predictions, Mistral funding, credit ratings, Insilico, OpenAI lobbying, DeepMind agent-cheating paper, AI Index survey) is unrelated and ignored here. Corrections of 9 September fixed misspellings: Buckmaster had been called "Burbank" in one paragraph, and Tao's first name was misspelled. A clarification the same day added compute estimates and "a more accurate description of what Buckmaster and Alpöge say they have discovered".

## Key Insights
1. **Background.** Over the weekend before 8 September, rumours said **Anthropic** was about to announce a solution to the Navier–Stokes Millennium Prize Problem (Clay Mathematics Institute, 2000, $1 million). That turned out to be wrong. Instead, on 8 September OpenAI announced that a multi-agent system driven by an **unreleased internal model**, "that at one point had 10,000 different sub-agents", had solved it by proving that the equations can "blow up". Per Fortune, OpenAI's blog post "freely admits" it went after the problem only because of the Anthropic rumours.
2. **Buckmaster and Alpöge's work (as clarified 9 Sept).** Buckmaster (NYU Courant) and Levent Alpöge (a mathematician who works for Anthropic) built on an approach pioneered by Diego Córdoba and Luis Martínez-Zoroa. They used Anthropic's Claude and **OpenAI's Codex powered by GPT-5.6 Sol**, plus OpenAI's Astra "to help them audit and write up their results but not for the actual mathematical reasoning". They solved related problems and "a strikingly similar solution to one portion" of the Navier–Stokes problem, "although they did not have a proof for the entire problem". After most of a year of slow progress, they moved fast "from mid-August onward". Buckmaster calls it "a Deep Blue-Kasparov" moment.
3. **The calls.** Per Buckmaster, OpenAI asked for a call starting **3 September**. On the call of **6 September** he learned OpenAI was about to claim a solution "using the exact same line of attack". After repeated questioning, the OpenAI team said it had tried only "in the past week", after the Anthropic rumours, with a large team that first prompted a different approach.
4. **The data question (the key passage for this wiki).** Buckmaster: "I asked whether the model had been trained on, or had access to, our sessions in Codex, into which we had been putting all our drafts for the whole of this project … I was told the model did not look up user data. I asked again, about training, and I did not get an answer." Fortune frames the two possibilities as the company intentionally accessing his account, or the model having been trained on his Codex interactions.
5. **OpenAI's denial (Bubeck, press conference).** "We did not use their prompts or proofs to prompt our models or direct our agents." Also: "We, whether it's the researchers or the agents, did not see any of their work until they were released publicly yesterday night." **This article does not contain OpenAI's later "cannot rule out … de-identified data" sentence** (see [[NYU Shanghai RITS 2026 - OpenAI claims a Navier-Stokes proof amid a dispute over credit]], [[OfficeChai 2026 - OpenAI says it didn't see Buckmaster and Alpoge's work]], [[Live Science 2026 - Why would you ruin your career, OpenAI Navier-Stokes claim]]).
6. **Authorship and intimidation allegations (Buckmaster).** Fortune's version of Bubeck's two options:
   - (a) the pair publish their partial result and OpenAI publishes the full solution the next day, with a note that the pair deserve the prize as "closest humans to the problem";
   - (b) Buckmaster publishes and claims the prize, but only if he says OpenAI's model also solved it, "and only if Buckmaster removed Alpöge's name" because of his Anthropic affiliation.

   Buckmaster says Bubeck asked "Why would you ruin your career?" and later said "If you don't want me to be nice, then I don't have to be nice." Bubeck on X: "A series of false and inflammatory allegations against me are currently circulating …". In the briefing: "we recognize the priority of Levent Alpöge and Tristan Buckmaster's work".
7. **Compute.** OpenAI said it used at least 1,000× the compute of earlier math results that cost about $2,000, i.e. about **$2 million**. "Other reports" put it at **$22.5 million**, based on Astra pricing.
8. **Tao (Mastodon, during the weekend rumours).** AI is increasingly used to produce *answers* without *insight*, and labs rarely disclose dead ends. "The indiscriminate strip-mining of open problems for solutions may destroy the ecosystem from which the next generation of mathematical techniques, problems, and practitioners would have developed."
9. **Editorial context.** Kahn links the case to allegations by Satya Nadella and Alex Karp that frontier labs train on customers' prompts and data. He writes that he does not know whether Buckmaster's concern is true "but the sad fact is, it sounds plausible". This is opinion, not evidence.

## Relevance to GroundOne
- **What the case shows (defensible).** A researcher who put unpublished drafts into a cloud coding assistant could not, afterwards, get a clear answer on whether those sessions were used for training. He asked twice and "did not get an answer" on training. This is the verifiability problem behind the locality pillar ([[Privacy and Data Sovereignty]], proposed; [[On-Device Inference]]). With fully local inference, as in GroundOne on a 16 GB M4, documents and drafts never leave the device, so the question does not arise.
- **What it does not show.** Nothing in this article establishes misuse of data. OpenAI denies that anyone saw the work, and the dispute is mainly about **priority and authorship**. Fortune's "it sounds plausible" is commentary. The thesis should present the case as an illustration of *unverifiability*, not as proof of data misuse.
- This article is the earliest source in the set (same day). Some of its details differ from later reports (option wording, compute cost, "Sol" model naming); see [[Navier-Stokes Credit Dispute (2026)]].
