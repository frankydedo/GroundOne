# Xu 2025 – Fast On-device LLM Inference with NPUs (llm.npu)

**Raw file**: `raw/local_inference/Xu 2025 - Fast On-device LLM Inference with NPUs (arXiv 2407.05858)_ingested.pdf`

## Overview
A peer-reviewed systems paper (ASPLOS '25; venue and DOI printed on the first page of the arXiv v2) presenting **llm.npu** (called mllm-NPU in earlier versions and in [[Xue 2024 - PowerInfer-2, Fast Large Language Model Inference on a Smartphone (arXiv 2406.06282)|PowerInfer-2]]'s citation), described as the first LLM inference system that offloads the **prefill** stage to a commercial mobile **NPU** (Qualcomm Hexagon). The motivation is that on-device LLM tasks such as UI automation or automated email reply have long prompts and very short outputs, so **prefill dominates latency**. Prefill is compute-bound, and mobile CPUs and GPUs lack the parallel compute for it. llm.npu changes the prompt and the model at three levels: fixed-size **chunk-sharing graphs** for variable-length prompts, **shadow outlier execution** (outlier activation channels run in float on the CPU while the NPU runs per-tensor INT8 MatMul), and **out-of-order subgraph scheduling** between CPU and NPU. It reports **22.4× faster prefill and 30.7× energy savings on average**, up to **32.8× end-to-end speedup**, and **more than 1,000 tokens/s prefill for a billion-sized model** (abstract).

## Key Insights
1. **Prefill is the bottleneck for on-device tasks (§2.1, Fig. 1).** With llama.cpp on a mobile CPU, prefill takes **88.3–98.8%** of end-to-end latency for chat summary, context-aware QA and UI automation. On a mobile GPU (TFLite) it still takes **54.2–91.7%**. Prompts are long (1,168–1,835 tokens for email reply on LongBench, 505–827 for DroidTask UI automation), while outputs average only **7.9 tokens** (LongBench) and **3.5 tokens** (DroidTask). Qwen1.5-1.8B on llama.cpp (CPU) needs **8.1 s** for one UI-automation step.
2. **Mobile NPUs are INT8 engines (§2.2, Tables 2–3).** Vendor INT8 peaks: Qualcomm Hexagon **73 TOPS**, MediaTek APU 790 60 TOPS, Huawei Ascend 16 TOPS, Google Edge TPU 4 TOPS. None of the listed NPUs supports per-group quantized MatMul, and none has an open-source SDK. On a Redmi K70 Pro, INT8 MatMul on the NPU is **4.5–5.8×** faster than CPU INT8 and **1.8–3.5×** faster than GPU FP16, but FP16 MatMul on the NPU is up to **159×** slower than CPU INT8 (§2.2 text; Table 3 lists NPU FP16 at 193–759× the NPU INT8 time). NPUs run at **500–750 MHz**, are more energy-efficient than CPUs and GPUs, and share physical memory with the CPU (no copies).
3. **Three gaps between LLMs and NPUs (§1, §2.3).** (i) NPUs need static shapes, and building plus optimising a graph is slow (Fig. 2: optimising takes 3.30 s for Qwen1.5-1.8B and 11.54 s for Gemma-2B). (ii) Per-group quantization, which LLMs need for outliers, must be split into sub-MatMuls on an NPU, with up to **10.7×** overhead. (iii) LayerNorm and attention stay in floating point and cannot run efficiently on the NPU.
4. **Techniques (§3).** Chunk-sharing reuses size-independent operators (e.g. FFN) across chunk graphs, which cuts memory by up to 75% (7.2 GB) for Qwen1.5-1.8B at a 1,024-token prompt (§3.2). Outliers sit in only **0.1–0.3%** of channels, so only "hot channel" weights are kept in CPU memory, and outliers in the 85% least important layers are pruned (§3.3, §4). Scheduling is an online heuristic that keeps the NPU, the critical path, busy (§3.4). Default chunk length is 256. Decoding still runs on the CPU backend (§4).
5. **Prefill results (§4.2, Fig. 14–15).** Setup: Redmi K70 Pro (Snapdragon 8 Gen 3, 24 GB) and Redmi K60 Pro (Snapdragon 8 Gen 2, 16 GB); Qwen1.5-1.8B, Gemma-2B, Phi-2-2.7B, LLaMA-2-7B and Mistral-7B. At 1,024 tokens on the K70 Pro, prefill latency falls by **18.17–38.4×** vs llama.cpp-CPU, **7.3×** vs MNN-CPU, **32.5–43.6×** vs MLC-GPU and **1.27–2.34×** vs TFLite-GPU. It is **3.28–5.32×** faster than PowerInfer-2's NPU prefill, but that comparison uses the numbers reported in the PowerInfer-2 paper, not a re-run (§4.1). Gains shrink for 64-token prompts because of padding.
6. **Energy (§4.2).** Measured from Android's `/sys/class/power_supply` every 100 ms, on the K60 Pro only. At 1,024 tokens, energy falls by **35.63–59.52×** vs llama.cpp-CPU, **35.21–59.25×** vs MLC-GPU and **1.85–4.32×** vs TFLite-GPU. At 64 tokens the savings are 10.38–14.12×, 10.38–17.79× and 3.22–3.67×. The authors explain this by all CPU cores running at full power during prefill, while the NPU runs at a low clock and draws the least power.
7. **End-to-end and accuracy (§4.3–4.4, Tables 5–6).** On LongBench (prompts of about 1.5k tokens, 2–11 output tokens), end-to-end speedups are **23.0–46.2×** vs llama.cpp-CPU and **1.27–2.03×** vs TFLite-GPU. The smaller gain over TFLite comes from llm.npu's unoptimised CPU decoder. Average accuracy is about **1% below FP16** on LAMBADA, HellaSwag, WinoGrande, OpenBookQA and MMLU. It is up to 32.9 points above SmoothQuant and 70.9 above K-Quant, and close to LLM.int8() (0.1% average loss). Memory use is up to **1.32×** llama.cpp's.
8. **Discussion (§5).** NPUs are preferred over mobile GPUs because GPUs are busy with rendering (resource contention). The authors expect integer arithmetic to stay dominant on NPUs even as FP16 support arrives. They ask for hardware with dynamic shapes, larger NPU caches and mixed-precision operands. The Hexagon NPU can address only a limited memory region (e.g. **4 GB**, §4), which can be smaller than the weights.

## Relevance to GroundOne
- **It accelerates exactly GroundOne's workload.** GroundOne's design (b) costs one compute-bound prefill and **zero decode steps** ([[Prefill and Decode]]). This paper is the clearest peer-reviewed evidence that a dedicated **NPU** speeds up the prefill phase and cuts its energy on consumer devices. Its motivating workloads (long context, 3.5–7.9 output tokens) are close to a verifier's, and GroundOne takes the extreme case of zero generated tokens. If decode is removed, the NPU's weak spot (the paper keeps decoding on the CPU) no longer matters. See [[Local Inference Hardware]].
- **Order-of-magnitude context, not a comparison.** llm.npu reaches more than 1,000 tokens/s of prefill for a ~1–2B model on a phone NPU in INT8. GroundOne on the M4 GPU (MLX, 8-bit) prefills about 2.8–3.0k tokens/s with 0.5B and about 450–470 tokens/s with 3B ([[Prefill and Decode]]). Models, precision, runtime and device all differ, so the thesis should not set these numbers side by side as a benchmark.
- **The M4's Neural Engine is unused.** MLX targets only the CPU and the GPU ([[Apple Silicon and MLX]]). The paper's energy result (1.85–4.32× below a mobile GPU at 1,024 tokens) suggests that a Core ML/ANE port of a prefill-only verifier could cut the measured **≈16 W / 33 J per answer** (3B; [[Energy of LLM Inference]]). That is a hypothesis for future work, not a result. The same paper lists the obstacles such a port would face: static shapes (variable-length documents would need fixed chunks), integer-only fast paths, closed SDKs, and float operators (LayerNorm, attention) that stay off the NPU.
- **Quantization can move decisions.** NPUs force per-tensor INT8 with outlier handling, and quantization choices changed accuracy by tens of points (Table 6). For a **calibrated** verifier, any NPU port would need its calibration refit and its decisions re-checked. One example in the wiki: an ANE port of a decision model had flipped 41 of 492 decisions because of a fused operator ([[FluidInference 2026 - d1-omni-600M Core ML model card]]). See [[Quantization]].
- **Privacy is the stated motivation.** The introduction motivates on-device LLMs with "rising privacy concerns" and "without giving away private data" (§1), which matches the thesis's locality pillar ([[Privacy and Data Sovereignty]]).
