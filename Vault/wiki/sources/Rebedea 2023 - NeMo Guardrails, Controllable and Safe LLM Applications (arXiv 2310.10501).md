# Rebedea 2023 – NeMo Guardrails: A Toolkit for Controllable and Safe LLM Applications with Programmable Rails

**Raw file**: `raw/demo/Rebedea 2023 - NeMo Guardrails, Controllable and Safe LLM Applications (arXiv 2310.10501)_ingested.pdf`

## Overview
NeMo Guardrails is an open-source toolkit (Apache 2.0) that places a **runtime proxy** between the user and the LLM to enforce **programmable rails**. These are developer-defined, model-independent, interpretable rules, in contrast to the **embedded rails** that alignment builds into the weights (§1, Fig. 1). The rules are written in **Colang**, a dialogue-flow modelling language interpreted by an event-driven dialogue manager. Two families of rails are described: **topical rails** (keep the conversation on designed flows) and **execution rails** (custom Python actions that check inputs and outputs, including fact-checking, hallucination and moderation) (§3).

## Key Insights
1. **Architecture (§3.1–3.2, Fig. 3).** Three LLM steps per turn: (1) map the user message to a *canonical form* with similarity-based few-shot prompting (examples retrieved from a vector index); (2) decide the next step, either from a matching developer flow or generated by the LLM; (3) generate the bot message conditioned on that step.
2. **Fact-checking rail (§3.3.1).** This rail targets RAG. It is a **binary entailment check by a prompted LLM**: "identify if the hypothesis is grounded and entailed in the evidence … Answer with yes/no", with the evidence and the *whole* bot response as the hypothesis. On a negative answer, the suggested handling is, for example, **abstaining**.
3. **Hallucination rail (§3.3.2).** For questions without retrieval, it uses SelfCheckGPT-style **self-consistency**: sample n answers, use n−1 as context and the n-th as hypothesis, and ask the LLM whether they agree.
4. **Moderation rails (§3.3.3).** Input moderation (jailbreak) and output moderation, both delegated to a well-aligned LLM. A response is returned only after passing both. Flows can replace a flagged response with a default text (§4.2, Fig. 4).
5. **Evaluation (§5).** *Topical rails* on Banking (231 samples, 77 intents) work even with falcon-7b-instruct and llama2-13b-chat. *Fact-checking* on MS MARCO, with hard negatives written by text-davinci-003: about **80% accuracy** for text-davinci-003 and gpt-3.5-turbo. *Moderation*: with both rails, gpt-3.5-turbo blocks close to 99% of harmful prompts (93% without rails) and only 2% of helpful ones. *Hallucination*: on **20** false-premise questions, the rail intercepts 70% for text-davinci-003 and raises gpt-3.5-turbo's deflection rate from 65% to 95%.
6. **Costs (§7.2).** The three chained calls cannot be batched, so latency and cost are **about 3×** a plain call. Few-shot prompts add a little more.
7. **Limitations (§7.1).** Programmable rails built on prompting are "not a perfect solution". They should complement embedded rails rather than stand alone, especially for safety. The authors plan customised (p-tuned) models for some rails instead of pure prompting. The evaluations are small: 20 questions for the hallucination rail, and MS MARCO negatives generated by an LLM.

## Relevance to GroundOne
- **The closest analogue of the demo's policy layer.** Like the demo, NeMo Guardrails separates *checking* from *deciding*. An output rail runs a check, and a developer-written flow decides what the user sees: block, replace with a default message, abstain. The demo's configurable policy (strict / lenient, τ, which classes block, max attempts, highlight / hide / abstain, audit log) is the same idea. So the existence of a policy layer is **not new**; what the demo adds is what the policy acts on ([[Guardrails and Policies]], [[Demo Application]]).
- **What GroundOne changes in the check.** The fact-checking rail is a **binary yes/no** prompt to a general LLM on the **whole response**, with no probability and therefore no tunable threshold, and it is reported at about 80% accuracy on a synthetic set. [[GroundOne]] gives **per-sentence three-way** verdicts (supported / contradicted / not mentioned) with **calibrated** probabilities and a dev-fitted τ, in one forward pass of a small local model. This allows sentence-level actions (rewrite only what failed, highlight only what is unsupported) and an explicit risk–coverage trade-off ([[Groundedness Verification]], [[Calibration]], [[Selective Prediction]]). Yes/no prompts also invite agreement bias (see [[Dhuliawala 2024 - Chain-of-Verification Reduces Hallucination in LLMs (arXiv 2309.11495)|CoVe]], Table 4).
- **No revise loop in the paper.** The rails in the paper block, deflect or replace. They do not feed the verdict back to the generator for a targeted rewrite. In the demo, the verify-and-revise step sits *before* the policy decides, which the paper does not describe ([[Verify-and-Revise Loops]]).
- **Cost argument.** The authors accept about 3× latency for three chained LLM calls and hope that smaller models will reduce it. A fine-tuned System One for the check is one concrete answer to that hope ([[System One Models]]).
