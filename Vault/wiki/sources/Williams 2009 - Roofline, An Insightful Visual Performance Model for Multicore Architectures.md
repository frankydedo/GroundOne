# Williams 2009 – Roofline: An Insightful Visual Performance Model for Multicore Architectures

**Raw file**: `raw/local_inference/Williams 2009 - Roofline, An Insightful Visual Performance Model for Multicore Architectures_ingested.pdf`

## Overview
The classic paper that introduced the **Roofline model**, a bound-and-bottleneck model that relates the attainable floating-point performance of a kernel to **off-chip memory traffic**. The authors assume that, "for the recent past and foreseeable future, off-chip memory bandwidth will often be the constraining resource" (§3). The model plots, on a log-log graph, attainable GFlop/s against **operational intensity** (Flops per byte of DRAM traffic). Each machine is drawn once as a flat compute roof plus a 45° bandwidth roof. Each kernel is a vertical line at its intensity. The paper demonstrates the model on four multicores (Intel Xeon Clovertown, AMD Opteron X4 Barcelona, Sun UltraSPARC T2+, IBM Cell QS20) and four kernels (SpMV, LBMHD, stencil, 3-D FFT). Note: the raw preprint is titled "Roofline: An Insightful Visual Performance Model for Floating-Point Programs and Multicore Architectures"; the CACM title drops "Floating-Point Programs".

## Key Insights
1. **Operational intensity (§3).** Operations per byte of DRAM traffic, measured *after* the cache hierarchy (between caches and memory, not between processor and caches). The authors prefer this to "arithmetic intensity", which in their terminology measures processor–cache traffic. Later LLM work (e.g. [[Yuan 2024 - LLM Inference Unveiled, Survey and Roofline Model Insights (arXiv 2402.16363)|Yuan 2024]]) uses "arithmetic intensity" for the same quantity.
2. **The Roofline equation (§3).** Attainable GFlops/s = min(Peak floating-point performance, Peak memory bandwidth × Operational intensity). Below the ridge the kernel sits on the slanted roof and is **memory-bound**; above it, on the flat roof, **compute-bound**. Peak bandwidth is the sustained bandwidth measured by optimised microbenchmarks (similar to STREAM), not the DRAM pin bandwidth.
3. **Ridge point (§3, §6.3).** The x-coordinate where the two roofs meet is the *minimum operational intensity needed to reach peak compute*. Example (Fig. 1a): the Opteron X2 has 17.6 GFlop/s peak (double precision) and 15 GB/s, with its ridge at 1.0 Flop/byte; at intensity 2 a kernel is compute-bound, at 1 memory-bound. Opteron X4 has its ridge at 4.4, Xeon 6.7, Cell 0.65, T2+ 0.33 (§6.3).
4. **Ceilings (§4, Fig. 2).** Lower roofs for missing optimisations: compute ceilings (ILP/SIMD, balanced multiply-add mix) and memory ceilings (unit-stride accesses, memory affinity, software prefetching). On the Opteron X2 they lie at 8.8 and 2.2 GFlop/s, and at 11, 4.8 and 2.7 GB/s. The gap between ceilings is the potential reward of that optimisation.
5. **Kernel intensities are low (Table 2, Table 4, §8).** SpMV 0.17–0.25, stencil 0.33–0.50, LBMHD 0.70–1.07, 3-D FFT 1.09–1.64 Flop/byte. Across the 16 kernel–machine pairs intensity ranged from 0.25 to 1.64 (median 0.60), while the x86 ridges were at 4.4 and 6.7 (35–55 Flops per 8-byte operand). 15 of 16 cases on Xeon and X4 were memory-bound (Table 4).
6. **Intensity is not fixed (§5).** It depends on caching (conflict and capacity misses lower it; compulsory misses set its maximum) and can **grow with problem size**, as for dense matrix and FFT. The advice is to raise a kernel's intensity before other optimisations.
7. **Generality (§7, §8).** The model is not limited to floating point or DRAM: the diagonal can be L2 or I/O bandwidth, and the y-axis any operation rate (e.g. exchanges per second for the FFT transpose, Fig. 5). It needs to be built once per machine, not once per kernel.

## Relevance to GroundOne
- **The vocabulary for the cost chapter.** "Prefill is compute-bound, decode is bandwidth-bound" is a roofline statement. The thesis can cite this paper for the model itself (attainable = min(peak, BW × intensity), ridge point) and LLM-specific works for the transformer numbers ([[Prefill and Decode]]).
- **Intensity of a linear layer in GroundOne (derived, not from the paper).** A weight matrix of size d_in × d_out applied to n tokens needs 2·n·d_in·d_out FLOPs and reads about d_in·d_out weight bytes (8-bit) plus 2·n·(d_in + d_out) activation bytes (fp16). So intensity ≈ 2·n·d_in·d_out / (d_in·d_out + 2·n·(d_in + d_out)):
  - **batch-1 decode (n = 1): ≈ 2 FLOP/byte** for any layer size (1 with fp16 weights);
  - **one GroundOne prefill of n = 500 tokens:** ≈ 310 (0.5B attention projection 896×896), ≈ 430 (0.5B FFN 896×4864), ≈ 510 (3B 2048×2048), ≈ 630 (3B FFN 2048×11008) FLOP/byte; the value grows with n and saturates near the layer dimension. Shapes from [[Qwen2.5]] `config.json`. This is the "intensity grows with problem size" case of §5: prefill is the dense-matrix case, decode the matrix-vector case.
- **The M4 on a roofline.** Apple gives the bandwidth (120 GB/s) but **no FLOPS figure** ([[Apple Silicon and MLX]]), so the flat roof cannot be drawn from specs. A lower bound comes from the thesis measurement: 0.5B prefill at ≈ 0.37 ms/token × about 2 × 0.49 G FLOPs/token gives ≈ 2.7 TFLOP/s achieved; 3B at 2.2 ms/token × 2 × 3.09 G gives ≈ 2.8 TFLOP/s (attention FLOPs ignored). The **effective ridge point is then at least about 22–23 FLOP/byte** (2.7–2.8 TFLOP/s ÷ 120 GB/s). Batch-1 decode (≈ 2) is an order of magnitude to the left (memory-bound); a GroundOne prefill (hundreds) is an order of magnitude to the right (compute-bound).
- **The model predicts the measured gap.** If decode costs weight bytes ÷ bandwidth and prefill costs 2N FLOPs ÷ achieved compute per token, the ratio is ≈ achieved FLOP/s ÷ (2 × bandwidth) ≈ 11–12× for 8-bit weights. The measured ratio is ≈ 16× (0.5B: 5.9 vs 0.37 ms/token) and ≈ 14× (3B: 31.8 vs 2.2 ms/token); decode overheads not in the model (KV-cache reads, kernel launches, sampling) can account for the excess. The weight-read floor alone is ≈ 4 ms (0.5B) and ≈ 26–27 ms (3B) per decoded token. This is the quantitative core of why a zero-token verifier is cheap ([[When System One Beats an LLM]]).
- **Ridge point as a hardware selection criterion.** §6.3.5 and §8 argue that the ridge point predicts how hard a machine is to use well. For GroundOne, a prefill-only workload benefits from more compute (it sits on the flat roof), while generative judges benefit from more bandwidth. Consistent with Apple's M5 note: TTFT gains 3.3–4.1×, generation only 1.19–1.27× ([[Local Inference Hardware]]).
- **Limits.** The paper concerns 2008 double-precision HPC kernels on CPUs/Cell; it says nothing about transformers, quantisation, unified memory or energy. All GroundOne numbers above are the thesis author's arithmetic, not results of the paper.
