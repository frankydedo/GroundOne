We analyze the EU AI Act by coding the foundation model requirements and comparing them to prior proposals for the Act.

The [EU AI Act](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=OJ:L_202401689) enters into force today: the Act is a watershed moment in the history of AI and technology policy, constituting the first comprehensive legislation on artificial intelligence. The Act is the byproduct of prescience and commitment by the European Union. The European Commission penned the initial White Paper on the AI Act in February 2020 and introduced the first draft of the Act in April 2021, and the EU came to political agreement on the legislation in December 2023. The EU AI Act sets the precedent for all subsequent AI policy: its efficacy will shape the future of artificial intelligence and society far beyond Europe.

The EU AI Act is a [key policy priority](https://crfm.stanford.edu/policy.html) for Stanford’s [Center on Research of Foundation Models](https://crfm.stanford.edu/). Specifically, the European Parliament introduced provisions on foundation models into the Act and came to [agreement](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/news/en/press-room/20230609IPR96212/meps-ready-to-negotiate-first-ever-rules-for-safe-and-transparent-ai) on the [Parliament position](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/TA-9-2023-0236_EN.pdf) in June 2023. A day later, we released an [initial assessment](https://crfm.stanford.edu/2023/06/15/eu-ai-act.html) of how major foundation model providers fared against the Parliament’s position and, in the subsequent months, we extensively engaged with EU legislators on the Act. As the trilogue negotiations came to a close at the end of 2023, we made public our thinking on how to design proportionate regulation by [tiering foundation models](https://crfm.stanford.edu/2023/11/18/tiers.html) and, more sharply, our [overall proposal to achieve compromise](https://crfm.stanford.edu/2023/12/01/ai-act-compromise.html) on the Act. Moving forward, we will continue to engage with the EU on the AI Act’s implementation through the AI Office and Scientific Panel.

This piece explores how foundation models are addressed by the EU AI Act. The Act’s requirements for foundation models and general-purpose AI will trigger one year from today, with the AI Office currently conducting a [stakeholder consultation](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/consultations/ai-act-have-your-say-trustworthy-general-purpose-ai) and designing [codes of practice](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/news/ai-act-participate-drawing-first-general-purpose-ai-code-practice). To understand the Act’s obligations, we furnish a rigorous **coding** of the Act’s requirements related to general-purpose AI. [Our coding](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1o2lRckbuv5M0nb9xpxWa028bwTN8_UT_pLTGeMvou5s/edit?usp=sharing) organizes the obligations based on whether they require information disclosures or substantive actions, target all developers or specific subsets, and categorize who receives any disclosed information (e.g. the public vs. the EU AI Office). We count 25 requirements that apply to 4 classes of general-purpose artificial intelligence. Most requirements are information disclosures where developers provide information to either the government or downstream firms. The few substantive requirements that compel a developer to take a particular action hinge on whether a model is designated as posing systemic risk. As of today, [estimates](https://epochai.org/data/large-scale-ai-models) indicate that 8 models meet the default criteria of 10^25 [training FLOPs](https://www.frontiermodelforum.org/updates/issue-brief-measuring-training-compute/).

Using our coding scheme, we **compare** the [Parliament proposal](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/TA-9-2023-0236_EN.pdf) of June 2023, the [Stanford proposal](https://crfm.stanford.edu/2023/12/01/ai-act-compromise.html) of December 2023, and the AI Act of July 2024. Each of these texts primarily centers on information disclosure: the Parliament position and the Stanford proposal emphasize public-facing disclosure, whereas the AI Act includes just one public-facing disclosure requirement that is specific to general-purpose AI. The Stanford proposal and the AI Act share a two-tiered approach and consider many similar criteria for setting the [threshold](https://crfm.stanford.edu/2023/11/18/tiers.html), yet the AI Act’s default criterion of compute diverges from the Stanford proposal’s focus on demonstrated market impact. Critically, while the AI Act includes many of the elements of the Stanford proposal, it lacks provisions for third-party researcher access to models (in contrast to the [EU Digital Services Act](https://algorithmic-transparency.ec.europa.eu/news/faqs-dsa-data-access-researchers-2023-12-13_en)) or adverse reporting (in contrast to the [G7 Code of Conduct](https://www.mofa.go.jp/files/100573473.pdf) and the [US National AI Advisory Committee recommendation](https://ai.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Recommendation_Improve-Monitoring-of-Emerging-Risks-from-AI-through-Adverse-Event-Reporting.pdf)). Third-party audits and adverse event reporting are [vital](https://hai.stanford.edu/policy-brief-ai-regulatory-alignment-problem) information gathering mechanisms for building the evidence base of [demonstrated harms](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.13843) from foundation models.

The legal text for the EU AI Act is an expansive [100+ page document](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=OJ:L_202401689) with 113 articles and 12 annexes that builds on a broad array of EU regulations on digital technology (e.g. GDPR on data privacy, DMA on market power, DSA on online platforms). The Act defines a *general-purpose AI model* as “an AI model, including when trained with a large amount of data using self-supervision at scale, that displays significant generality and is capable to competently perform a wide range of distinct tasks regardless of the way the model is placed on the market and that can be integrated into a variety of downstream systems or applications. This does not cover AI models that are used before release on the market for research, development and prototyping activities”.

The obligations for general-purpose AI are organized under Chapter V: General-Purpose AI Models (Articles 51 – 56) along with three supporting annexes (Annex XI – XIII). Obligations for general-purpose AI vary significantly based on whether a model is classified as posing *systemic risk* (Articles 51 – 52). While the obligations for general-purpose AI take effect on August 2, 2025, the EU AI Office is tasked (Article 56) with preparing *codes of practice* by May 2, 2025 to facilitate compliance.


We [code](https://docs.google.com/spreadsheets/d/1o2lRckbuv5M0nb9xpxWa028bwTN8_UT_pLTGeMvou5s/edit?usp=sharing) the AI Act’s requirements on general-purpose AI to increase organization and clarity. To start, we collated the requirements with short-form descriptors. For each requirement, we classified it as either a *disclosure* requirement, meaning information must be disclosed by the provider of a general-purpose AI model to some entity, or a *substantive* requirement, meaning the provider must implement some practice. For each disclosure requirement, we further coded the *recipient* to whom the information must be disclosed, which may include (i) the EU and national governments, (ii) downstream providers that integrate the model into their AI system, or (iii) the public. And, finally, we coded every requirement for the *scope* of providers to which it applies, which may include (i) all providers, (ii) providers of open models without systemic risk, (iii) providers of non-open models without systemic risk, or (iv) providers of models with systemic risk.

**Takeaways.**

- **Emphasis on disclosure.** The majority of the requirements are disclosures to the EU and national governments or to downstream providers: in most cases, the same information is required to be disclosed to both parties.
- **Minimal public-facing disclosure.** There is only one public-facing disclosure requirement: providers must publicly disclose a [sufficiently detailed summary of training data](https://openfuture.eu/wp-content/uploads/2024/06/240618AIAtransparency_template_requirements-2.pdf). Although it is the sole public-facing disclosure, this could meaningfully increase transparency given the systemic opacity of foundation model developers with respect to data, as shown by the [May 2024 Foundation Model Transparency Index](https://crfm.stanford.edu/fmti).
- **Substantive requirements are primarily for models posing systemic risk.** General-purpose AI models that are designated as carrying systemic risk face additional requirements that are primarily substantive. Their providers must implement model evaluation, risk mitigation, incident reporting, and cybersecurity protection. In contrast, general-purpose AI models that do not carry systemic risk have just a single substantive requirement related to adherence to copyright law.
- **Several models may be designated as systemic.** Based on [estimates from Epoch](https://epochai.org/data/large-scale-ai-models?view=table), 8 models (Gemini 1.0 Ultra, Llama 3.1-405B, GPT-4, Mistral Large, Nemotron-4 340B, MegaScale, Inflection-2, Inflection-2.5) from 7 developers (Google, Meta, OpenAI, Mistral, NVIDIA, ByteDance, Inflection) would meet the default systemic risk criterion of being trained using at least 10^25 FLOPs.
- **Alternative criteria to compute are suggested.** Given [critiques of compute](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.05694v1) as the basis for [tiering foundation models](https://crfm.stanford.edu/2023/11/18/tiers.html), Annex XIII identifies alternative metrics the AI Office may consider to designate models as posing systemic risk, such as data properties, evaluation results, number of business users, and number of registered end-users.
- **Partial exemption for [open models](https://crfm.stanford.edu/open-fms/).** Article 53 indicates that many of the obligations “do not apply to providers of AI models that are released under a free and open-source licence”, but that “this exception shall not apply to general-purpose AI models with systemic risks”. As other jurisdictions work to develop [policy for open foundation models](https://www.ntia.gov/press-release/2024/ntia-supports-open-models-promote-ai-innovation), including the US, a key consideration will be the role of licenses, given that models with open weights often are *not* released with [Open Source Initiative-compliant open-source licenses](https://opensource.org/osd).

To understand the evolution of the AI Act, we compare it to prior proposals made during the AI Act legislative process. In particular, given our focus on foundation models and general-purpose AI, we consider the European Parliament’s [negotiated position](https://www.europarl.europa.eu/doceo/document/TA-9-2023-0236_EN.pdf) from June 2023. This is the first formal EU position to introduce the subject of foundation models. And we also consider [our own position](https://crfm.stanford.edu/2023/12/01/ai-act-compromise.html) from December 2023. To compare both of these proposals to the AI Act, we code them using the same coding scheme that we describe above.


- **Transparency for whom?** All three texts share an emphasis on transparency, but the Parliament and Stanford proposals focus on transparency to the public whereas the AI Act includes only one public-facing disclosure. This means it is much less obvious how the information that companies disclose will translate into action and whether it will yield substantially greater accountability, as only a small subset of actors will have insight into these disclosures.
- **Multi-tier approach.** The Parliament proposal treats all providers equally, whereas the Stanford proposal and the AI Act share the same structure of two tiers and a (near) exemption. However, the Stanford proposal argues for demonstrated market impact as the primary basis for tiering, which the AI Act considers as an alternative to its default criterion of training compute measured in FLOPs.
- **Disclosures.** The disclosures, putting aside who receives the information, largely agree across all three proposals. The Stanford proposal and the AI Act match exactly in simplifying the training data summary requirement to not specifically consider copyright, unlike the Parliament proposal.
- **No third-party auditing access or adverse event reporting.** The Stanford proposal, based in part on [Guha et al. (2023)](https://hai.stanford.edu/policy-brief-ai-regulatory-alignment-problem) and recommendations of the [US National AI Advisory Committee](https://ai.gov/wp-content/uploads/2023/12/Recommendation_Improve-Monitoring-of-Emerging-Risks-from-AI-through-Adverse-Event-Reporting.pdf), recommends model access for independent third-party auditors and adverse event reporting from parties beyond model developers (e.g. users). These elements do not appear in the AI Act’s requirements for models with systemic risks.
- **Terminology.** Both proposals use the term “foundation model,” whereas the AI Act uses the term “general-purpose AI model,” though all three share a similar definition that closely aligns with the original definition of [Bommasani et al. (2021)](https://crfm.stanford.edu/report.html) and the US definition in the [Biden Executive Order](https://www.whitehouse.gov/briefing-room/presidential-actions/2023/10/30/executive-order-on-the-safe-secure-and-trustworthy-development-and-use-of-artificial-intelligence/). The upper tier in the Stanford proposal refers to “high-impact” models, whereas the AI Act designates models “with systemic risk,” though it makes reference to “high-impact capabilities”.
- **Penalties.** The AI Act and the Parliament proposal include penalties for non-compliance: the AI Act’s penalties are greater and stand at 3% of total worldwide turnover in the preceding financial year, or (if greater) 15 million Euros. This is an increase over the penalties of 2% of total worldwide turnover or 10 million Euros in the Parliament position.
